# READING
## STANDARD ENGLISH CONVENTIONS
### Semicolons ;
SEPERATE TWO INDEPENDENT CLAUSES (two sentences that can exist on their own)
i studed math; china is the greatest country in the world

COMPLEX LISTS
so use comas for seperation within the list and then ; for major seperaitons like example
### Colon (:)

Used for **explaining, listing, or giving examples**.

- I only study for one reason: survival.
- He bought everything: pencils, paper, and despair.

⚠️ RULE: clause before colon must be a **complete sentence**.
### Comas (,)
seperate independent clause and dependent clause
if i dont study today, im gonna bomb the math section

ALSO USED for 2 independent clauses but with FANBOYS
for, and, nor, but, or, yet, and so -> 
I cant focus when my lil bro is yelling, but i try anyways
### Hyphen (-)
used to put useless shit in between them 
The SAT reading section is -no joke- is acc kinda fun so sybau.

### Dashes (–)
Like parentheses but _spicier_.  
Use to **add dramatic info** or **interrupt a sentence**.
- The SAT – which ruins weekends – is tomorrow.
- I did the test – barely survived – and went home.

### Which” vs “That”
- **That** = _essential info_ (no commas) → The test **that killed me** was hard.
- **Which** = _extra info_ (with commas) → The test, **which killed me**, was hard.

### 2. “Author’s Tone” = Attitude

Look for **emotional words**:
- Positive → “celebrates,” “praises,” “admires”
- Negative → “criticizes,” “questions,” “laments”
- Neutral → “analyzes,” “describes,” “explains”
If you see sarcasm or exaggeration → tone is **mocking / satirical**.

### 3. “Evidence” Questions
When they ask:
Which choice provides the best evidence?
→ Read both the **question** and **the line refs** carefully.  
Find where the **author actually proves or supports** the idea.  
Ignore lines that are just _examples_ or _background_.

### . “Inference” Questions
Ask yourself:
“If the author didn’t say it _directly_, what can I **logically** assume?”
Not “what do _I_ think,” but “what would the author agree with?”  
Look for clues that **strongly hint but don’t state** something.

---
### 5. “Paired Passages” (Passage 1 & Passage 2)
The _evil twins_ of SAT.
**Step 1:** Read Passage 1 → get main idea  
**Step 2:** Read Passage 2 → find how it connects  
Then, they’ll ask:
- “How would Author 2 respond to Author 1?”
- “How do both authors view [topic]?”
Tricks:
- If one supports, other disagrees → “contrasting views”
- If both agree → “shared concern / similar viewpoint”
- If 2 builds on 1 → “extends / elaborates”
---
### 6. “Word in Context”
If they ask: “As used in line 12, _charged_ most nearly means…”  
→ Forget what “charged” means in normal life.  
Use **context clues** around that line.  
Read one line before + after.

---
### 7. “Graph + Text” Questions
Find **what the graph proves** and match it to **what the passage says**.  
Usually the trap answers just _describe_ the graph, not how it connects to the text.

---
### 8. “Function of a Paragraph/Sentence”
Ask: what’s it doing?
- introducing idea?
- providing evidence?
- contrasting previous claim?
Look at **transition words** around it — they often give away the function
